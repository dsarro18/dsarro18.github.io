Old Thoughts

November 3, 2026

Before Fragments, there was another blog. From 2021 to 2023 I wrote almost daily about whatever was on my mind: markets, sports, politics, business, and the occasional deeper question. Most of it was noise. Some of it was not.

These are ten pieces that still hold up, lightly cleaned up but left in the spirit they were written. Reading them back, many of the ideas I write about here started there, just rougher around the edges.

1. The Struggle for Creativity (September 2021)

I have watched an absurd amount of YouTube over the past year: LEGO reviews, movies, languages, politics, sports, tech launches. You name it, I have seen at least a preview of it. Part of that is because YouTube is addicting by design. It learns your preferences as you scroll and serves content personalized for you and no one else in the world. Incredible technology, and it is limiting you.

Think about it on a deeper level. Do the companies behind the appliances at the store, the new Tesla, or the trendy app on your phone truly want innovation? Sure, but only from themselves, not from competitors and certainly not from you. Their goal is a product that makes you say "wow" and open your wallet. YouTube is no different. It wants you browsing and watching ads to gather data on your habits and keep you on the app as long as possible. Every product wants you hooked, not actively engaged. Big difference.

That is the constant struggle for any inventor or entrepreneur: how do you build something addicting while keeping users from using that same creativity against you?

We used mental imagery at Villanova for baseball, and I think it is badly underappreciated. Picturing success takes you back to being a kid daydreaming, and once creativity is triggered, ideas start to flow. Give yourself fifteen minutes a day to dream again. None of it may come true, but nothing will come from lining up for the newest iPhone every twelve months either.

2. Where Modern Marketing Fails (September 2021)

As a kid I loved technology: jailbreaking iPhones, flashing custom Android ROMs, waiting for every launch event. And none of that era happens without one man, Steve Jobs.

What Jobs did with the original iPhone changed how I looked at the world. He made a product people did not know they wanted, then made them crave it. He did not just create demand. He created dependency. He turned a want into a need.

This is where modern advertising fails. Commercials today are either boring or so ridiculous you wonder who approved them. Everyone wants exposure, but nobody creates dependency. Everyone knows the GEICO commercials. Does anyone actually want insurance after watching one? I doubt it. I remember the early iPhone ad where a guy "drank" milk from his phone after tapping a little square called an app. I immediately thought, "I need this." Apple hooked me in an ecosystem no one else could build, and probably no one ever will again.

What is even more impressive is that Apple sells a premium product to everyone, from CEOs to ten-year-olds, without the quality wavering. Permanently in style. It all started with a company selling need rather than want. Maybe if more advertisers followed suit, we would not hit "skip" on every ad we see.

3. Unethical Business? (September 2021)

Designing my own website has been one of the best learning exercises of my life. For the first time, nobody is forcing me to use my brain; I get to build what I envision. Every detail matters: color, texture, shape, placement. And there is a constant pull between how it looks and what you want the user to do.

That pull raises a question. Is it unethical to place a button in a certain spot, with a certain color, knowing it will steer the user? Or to choose wording because it triggers a certain response? At what point does the user become exploited? Nobody forces you to click. It is your money and your choice.

Facebook's own research showed Instagram was harming the mental health of young girls. The same defense applies: nobody makes a young girl download the app and compare herself to others. But is that really an answer? Does the company share any blame? Do games built around in-app purchases share blame for kids' spending habits? Where does the user's responsibility end and the company's begin?

I am still building my website, and I believe it will do real good. But that grey area will always exist, and I will keep asking what role I play in the game called business.

4. The Addiction to "Gambling" (September 2021)

Not all gambling is the same. We associate it with addiction and failure, but at its core gambling is just risk versus return. And everything is a gamble. Driving, eating, drinking, sleeping, spending. Each involves a risk/return judgment and some capital on the line, whether that capital is cash, your body, or your life.

Yet not all gambles feel the same. Tens of thousands of Americans die in car accidents every year, and we still drive to work without a second thought. Now suppose I offer you a coin flip: heads, you win $1,000,000; tails, you pay me $10,000. Many people answer instantly in one direction or the other. Both reflexes are wrong. Too much risk is dangerous, and total risk aversion means a sheltered life where you miss what others seize. The answer is to take the bet. The odds are even and the upside is a hundred times the downside, unless losing $10,000 means you cannot make rent.

We make decisions like this every day when the odds are overwhelmingly in our favor. That is how we should live. But sports betting and speculative trading flip the logic: millions of people bet daily where the odds are heavily against them, because they stare at the payoff and ignore the probability. I understand the pull. I have owned stocks and bet on sports, and I know the thrill of winning and the despair of losing.

A whole market exists of people addicted to taking risk regardless of odds. Whether that is a problem depends entirely on what you are putting on the line.

5. Renaissance Work (October 2021)

Final Jeopardy the other night asked about a Renaissance man whose actions in 1503 shaped his most famous work a decade later. The answer was Niccolò Machiavelli, author of The Prince. It should be required reading for every college student.

What fascinates me is how much from that era still shapes the world six centuries later: Michelangelo, da Vinci, Gutenberg, Luther, Columbus. Compare that to our time. We have Jobs, Bezos, Musk, and Zuckerberg. All have achieved enormous wealth and fame. Will their legacies hold up for 100 years, let alone 600? I doubt it.

Which raises the uncomfortable question: are we getting dumber? Pick up a classic from 80 or 100 years ago and look at the grammar, the syntax, the vocabulary. It reads like another world, yet your grandparents lived in it, many without a high school education. What is driving the decline, if there is one? What work from our era will anyone know in 600 years? Will my descendants still read The Prince or travel to Florence to see the David?

6. A Small Yet Massive Change (November 2021)

I remember when an iPhone app cost $0.99 and that felt like a lot. I remember new games like Call of Duty costing $59.99, and you saved all year for the November release. That was simply how the market worked.

Then Clash of Clans and Fortnite changed everything. The model was simple: free to download and play, with extras that cost money. The extras did not change your ability to play; they helped you progress faster or look different. Genius.

Free lets everyone try it. Free increases exposure because friends can all play together. And free makes it easy to justify little purchases, $5 here and $10 there, because after all, the game was "free." In the end those companies made far more than $0.99 or $59.99 per player.

What baffles me is how long it took. Comparative pricing drives purchasing everywhere in business, yet gaming clung to fixed price points for years, selling premium products to kids spending their own money. They stopped looking at threats and changes around them. Once Fortnite proved the model, every major game copied it within a couple of years and the industry changed forever.

7. Intelligence is a Privilege, Not a Gift (November 2021)

A friend and I were talking about financial scandals: Enron, the 2008 bailouts where executives took bonuses out of relief money. Dishonest, scummy, destructive. Countless lives have been ruined by money throughout history.

Part of me judges them on morality. Another part quietly admits they are brilliant. It is naïve to think every intelligent person acts for good. We judge through a movie lens, good versus evil with no grey area, and real life has never worked that way.

Later that day I rewatched Sam Raimi's Spider-Man 2. Doctor Octavius says intelligence is not a privilege but a gift, and should be used for the good of mankind. I think he has it backwards. Intelligence is a privilege, and privileges can be abused. The people behind Enron could manipulate billions of dollars, run national companies, and convince everyone they were right. Things the average person cannot do. We should not idolize them, but we should study how they did it, so the next ones are stopped at the start.

Intelligence is the path to fortune or deceit. Choose wisely.

8. There is Beauty in Finite Time (July 2022)

If everything were infinite, the world would be boring, miserable, and predictable. The value of vacations, friendships, seasons, and life itself is that they end someday, and at the start we do not know when. As time passes we begin to see the end coming, but in the beginning, no. You enter each new adventure excited, with no end in sight.

That is what gives it all purpose. We obsess over deadlines and knowing everything in advance. Take a step back, enjoy the ride, and let yourself be both upset and grateful when it ends. There is no better way to live.

9. Nostalgia for What Exactly? (September 2022)

Nostalgia is a drug. The brain responds to it like a pill or a drink. Some people chase it forever without ever being satisfied; others seem immune.

But I am not talking about missing your own past. I mean nostalgia for times you never lived. I look at the 1980s as an era of American prosperity: the music, the sports, a society that by and large was improving, styles that influenced the whole world. Can I miss that? Am I entitled to? I only know it through videos, the internet, and my parents' memories. How can I miss something I never experienced?

As society feels more divided and stagnant despite the constant push for "progress," what comes next? Did people in the 1980s miss the '70s, or the '50s? I am fascinated by the line between nostalgia and pure fantasy. Will anyone miss the 2010s? Will there be a Stranger Things for the 2020s? A band idolized the way the Beatles or Queen were? We will see, but I have my doubts.

10. The Struggle to Create (May 2023)

Building any business starts with a simple question: what do you build? But that is not really step one. Step one is asking yourself honestly: am I creating a solution, or a problem?

Most people immediately say they want to solve problems. That is looking at the world through Gandhi's eyes. Businesses exist to create demand, ideally demand that never ends. Who builds a company to solve a problem and then cease to exist?

Take Meta. Its products are tied to mental health problems, self-esteem issues, and biased algorithms. Does it care? It is making billions, and the occasional donation does not change that. So is it moral to create problems you then sell the solution to? Or to let problems persist because they are profitable? Nobody seems to be debating it. We are all on our phones anyway.
