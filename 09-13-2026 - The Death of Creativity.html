The Death of Creativity

September 13, 2026

I am aware there are a billion articles on this topic already, and my voice is yet another in a crowded room full of armchair quarterbacks. But I want to and feel obligated to share my opinions and thoughts as a 28-year-old searching for his next venture post-MBA graduation.

This will not be the satirical angle of American Psycho, nor the Pink Floyd Another Brick in the Wall anthem highlighting uniformity. This angle will highlight how important the relationship is between access to information and risk-taking, along with what it means for society when these correlate.

The previous post focused on what true risk and consequence is, and the ability to take risk with other people's money. Some of you may find it shocking that it is easier to lose OTHER people's money than your own... for those unaware, well, welcome to society.

This entry is a continuation of a related topic centered around problem solving to perfection.

Take tennis for example. Just 5% of players within the ATP Top 100 have a one-handed backhand shot. All the courts play nearly the same now with a focus on long rallies and baseline play. Everyone serves the same, eats the same, trains nearly the same, and uses equipment of the same materials. The differentiator is raw physicality and strength, along with who can stay healthy. The result? The same 2-3 guys in every final who are playing identical tennis. Women's is no different, just shorten from 5 sets to 3.

What is happening here?

You have coaches all watching the same analytics, using the same AI models, teaching the same techniques, watching competitors all doing the same thing. The result is Taylor Fritz playing like Carlos Alcaraz and getting nowhere result wise relative to Carlos. Fans all watch the same product, paying inflated prices for the "experience," and long for the days of Nadal, Federer, and Djokovic.

This trend can be extrapolated into every major sport. Baseball's motto is throw hard and either strikeout or homer. Football is the same routes, same roster construction, same playcalling, with stadiums all merging into indoor turf. Soccer is 4-4-2 and play out of the back. Yawn.

Part of the human experience is the drive to innovate, to advance, to reach new frontiers. This requires risk and venturing into the unknown. When everything is "solved" or "perfectly efficient" then why would you deviate? Where is the reward?

For a better illustration, let's analyze two topics that illustrate this concept from different perspectives.

The first is investing. The "method" to wealth creation and growth for the past 15-20 years is buy ETFs, park cash in money markets or CDs, and wait. Buy a home, contribute the employer match to your 401k, and wait. The result is 7-10% annual returns, beating inflation of 3%. An asset portfolio worthy of retirement at 60. Capital generating (paper) gains and additional capital.

Side note, this also beats 99% of hedge funds' performances for good measure. It feels like we have solved investing...

The second is human resource allocation. Between food storage and allocation, along with increasing renewable energies and efficiency, Malthus has not only been proven wrong, but he is a laughingstock 200 years later. We have modified foods to sizes, quality, and durability beyond our wildest imagination. Oil no longer appears to be extinct due to waste (political turmoil, yes) and we can access energy in virtually every part of the world (again, politics are the bigger obstacle). Again, seems like we have solved these issues as well.

Is solving energy shortages good? Yes. How about food scarcity? Again, yes. How about sports, chess, poker, travel routes and planning, or film making? These topics, well, I don't know.

Losing the ability to innovate, to take risks, to see a reward worthy of the risk is rapidly deteriorating. Education is already bracing for a generation reliant on AI. I mean, why learn about obscure topics when Claude can give you them in 3 seconds...

What is equally lacking is the incentive to act. This is true across nearly every venture. The incentive to work, to stay at your job, to play sports, to hang out with friends, to learn a new skill... yet it is rarely discussed. Society has blamed the wrong party for why "comfort" is not only the new norm but the preferred option.

Two last points to illustrate all the points above that can be summarized into one scenario.

Take food delivery. It costs MORE to drive to the restaurant, pick up my order, and drive home. Factor in time, and it is a no-brainer. Where is the restaurant's incentive to get me to go in and sit down or drive and pick it up myself? Free meal? Discount? ANYTHING!

How about dating? Is sex that much of an incentive, let alone marriage, to spend time and money on dates, trips, events, time, etc.? What are WE doing to attract each other, and what are dating apps / sites doing to get us invested?

To me, this is a two-part problem. Once we "solve" or "perfect" an idea, process, or concept, there is no reason to take risks. Marginal utility exists for a reason. But the second part is when those same people complain about stagnation. Why is there no pressure to enhance incentives to TAKE those risks again?

The result is a stalemate that does not appear to be changing anytime soon.
